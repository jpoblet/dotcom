## Context

Rillet makes accounting software for accounting teams. I joined in November 2021 as employee number six and the company's first designer. When I arrived there was nothing to speak of: no landing page, and just a few basic features that weren't consistent with each other. The job was clear: create a product and a brand that could scale far beyond what we had.

We built everything from zero: a contract builder with smart revenue recognition and planning, journal entries booked automatically, analytics and reporting. Towards the end of my time there we started building AI into the product. While I was there, Rillet went from no clients to more than 40, and reached $0.5M in annual recurring revenue.

## My role

I designed the product, the logo and the brand, the dashboards and the design system. For most of my time I was the only designer, working with up to nine developers at once. I hired a second designer, but that role only lasted about four months, so the setup stayed the same: one designer, a lot of engineers and a product that had to keep shipping.

## Stopping to redesign

At first we had no customers and a very basic platform that we were building bit by bit. At some point the design was all over the place, and the product needed a redesign before it could scale. I created a vision for the platform's UI, with a roadmap for the first few months, and proposed something bold: stop building new features and put everyone on the front end and the redesign.

I took it directly to the CEO, the CTO and the engineers, and sold them on it. We all agreed it was the right call before we started building more complex functionality. We did it, and that foundation is what the platform scaled on. It still holds strong today.

## Working with the founders

I worked very closely with both founders. With the CEO I planned the strategy for the most important features, and we brainstormed the next steps of the experience together. With the CTO I stayed aligned on how each experience affected development: I always kept in mind that how fast we shipped also depended on how hard my designs were to build.

## Process

With one designer and nine engineers, the obvious risk was that I'd become the bottleneck, and that whatever got built without me would pile up design debt. So I changed my role. Instead of designing every screen myself, I became a design reviewer and the maintainer of the design system.

- **A robust design system first.** I invested early in a solid system of components and patterns, good enough that engineers could build screens from it with confidence.
- **Kick-off together.** For each feature, I sat down with the assigned developer, went through the ticket and sketched the solution together as rough wireframes.
- **Engineers build, without mock-ups.** The developer built the feature straight from the design system and those wireframes. I didn't design a single final screen.
- **Review it live.** Once the feature ran locally, we reviewed it together, tweaked whatever needed tweaking, designed and documented any new components the system needed, and pushed it to production.

That let us ship complex features quickly, without me holding anything up and without creating design debt. Looking back, it was an "agentic" way of working before the AI era: I set up the system and the rules, and others produced the work within them.

## Outcome

When I left, I left behind a solid, user-friendly accounting platform, and a way of working built on a robust design system that let engineers create UIs and solutions themselves, following the patterns and using the components I'd made.

The framework, the logo and brand, the dashboard screens and the design system I put in place stayed almost unchanged for years after I left. Rillet only rebranded and started moving to a new visual direction in October 2026.

The company has done very well since. After a seed round of about $13.5M in mid-2024, while I was still there, it raised a $25M Series A led by Sequoia in 2025, a $70M Series B in August 2025 and a $100M Series C led by ICONIQ at a $1B valuation in August 2026: about $208M raised in total, with investors including Sequoia, Andreessen Horowitz and ICONIQ. It's the biggest impact I've had on anything I've been part of: the brand and the platform I created are the ones the company went on to raise over $200M with.

## Why I left

Once the design system and the framework were in place, we reached a point where the go-to-market team wasn't getting enough traction to keep the ARR steady. Some of the major feature work was paused, so there wasn't going to be enough design work to justify my salary. We decided to part ways. After a good run there, I also wanted to work on a different kind of project, in a different sector.
